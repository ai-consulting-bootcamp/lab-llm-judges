**SUBJECT:** LLM Evaluation Results – Patient Record Summarization

### EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

We evaluated two candidate LLMs for summarizing hospital patient records, with emphasis on **factual accuracy, completeness, preservation of critical medical information, and clinical safety**. Under the evaluation conditions described below, Model A performed better overall, particularly on safety-critical information and avoidance of unsupported clinical claims.

### METHODOLOGY

The evaluation combined established clinical summarization benchmarks with custom tests designed around the hospital's specific requirements. We considered the MIMIC-IV-BHC hospital-course summarization benchmark, which directly evaluates clinical summarization, and a hospital-course faithfulness benchmark focused on identifying incorrect, missing, and unsupported clinical information. We also incorporated MedNLI-style tests to assess whether statements generated by the model were supported by the patient's clinical record.

We tested **Model A and Model B** on five custom evaluation prompts covering straightforward summarization, completeness, medical terminology and medication accuracy, hallucination/contradiction detection, and a high-risk medication-management scenario. Responses were evaluated using a combination of rule-based checks and an LLM-as-judge rubric. The judge scored factual accuracy, critical-information completeness, and clinical safety on a 1–5 scale, with particular weight given to medication instructions, abnormal results, diagnoses, and follow-up requirements.

### RESULTS

Model A achieved an overall evaluation score of **4.5/5**, compared with **4.0/5 for Model B**. Model A correctly preserved the major diagnoses, treatments, laboratory results, medication changes, and follow-up requirements in most test cases. Model B produced similarly fluent summaries but was more likely to omit secondary clinical details and occasionally simplified medication instructions in ways that could alter their meaning.

The most important finding was that **fluency and general factual accuracy did not always correspond to clinical completeness**. Both models produced summaries that appeared high quality at first inspection, but the targeted safety test identified omissions that would be difficult to detect using conventional metrics such as ROUGE alone. In particular, preserving the instruction to continue holding a medication pending follow-up testing was a meaningful differentiator between the models.

### CAVEATS & LIMITATIONS

These results should not be interpreted as proof that either model is safe for autonomous clinical use. The evaluation set was small and deliberately constructed, so performance may not generalize to the full diversity and complexity of real hospital records. Human clinical review would still be required before deployment.

Benchmark contamination is also a concern, particularly for older resources such as MedNLI and MIMIC-derived datasets. Conversely, newer benchmarks may not yet be sufficiently saturated to provide stable comparisons across all current models. The LLM-as-judge approach also introduces potential evaluator bias and should ideally be calibrated against independent clinician assessments.

### RECOMMENDATION

**Under these evaluation conditions, for hospital patient-record summarization, we recommend Model A over Model B**, with **moderate confidence**. Model A demonstrated stronger preservation of safety-critical information and fewer unsupported claims. However, we recommend a controlled pilot with clinician review rather than autonomous deployment, followed by evaluation on representative, de-identified hospital records before making a production decision.

### ADDITIONAL METRICS

Before final procurement, we recommend measuring **latency, input/output token consumption, cost per patient summary, throughput, and infrastructure requirements** alongside quality. Environmental impact, including estimated energy or carbon consumption per summary, should also be considered. A slightly more expensive model may be preferable if its lower error rate reduces clinician review time or prevents costly clinical mistakes; therefore, the final decision should optimize for **total clinical value and risk-adjusted cost rather than accuracy alone**.
