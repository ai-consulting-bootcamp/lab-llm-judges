# Reflection

## 1. What would change if the client's data were in French?

I would create the evaluation from native French client material rather than mechanically translate English prompts. Fluent consultants would review instructions, anchors, outputs, and gold annotations across francophone contexts. I would test whether numbers, register, negation, and uncertainty survive cross-language workflows. English benchmarks could inform categories, but their thresholds would require recalibration.

Challenges include fewer domain reference sets, translation artifacts, different professional-tone expectations, and judge preference for English-like phrasing. I would compare French and bilingual judging, hide model identity, and measure agreement with two human reviewers. A native-domain expert would adjudicate high-impact disagreements.

## 2. How would you respond to “Is this model AGI-level?”

“AGI-level” is not an operational acceptance criterion and has no agreed test. Strong scores show bounded performance, not general intelligence. I would ask which capabilities matter: evidence preservation, instruction following, tool use, multilingual work, planning, or safe behavior under novel inputs.

We could test those capabilities with held-out, adversarial, multilingual, multi-step, and tool-use tasks against human baselines, but conclusions would remain scoped. Contamination, prompt sensitivity, judge bias, and production differences prevent a defensible binary claim. I would report task reliability and residual risk instead.

## 3. What is the one thing that cannot be evaluated without a human, and why?

I would not delegate the final judgment of whether a recommendation is appropriate and dignified for affected communities. A factually correct answer can still be paternalistic, ignore power dynamics, or be politically unacceptable. An LLM lacks accountable, lived participation in the context; rules cannot enumerate every relational harm.

Automation would filter clear failures, followed by a consultant, domain owner, and—where feasible—an affected-group representative. They would score a stratified sample, review every high-impact case, record rationales, and add adjudicated examples to calibration. Human review would be a governed decision point.
