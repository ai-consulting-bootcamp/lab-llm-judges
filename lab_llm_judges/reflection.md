# Reflection

## Question 1: What would change if the client's data was in French?

If NovaMart's customer base communicated primarily in French, the evaluation design would need to change at multiple levels. First, none of the three benchmarks I audited would apply — MT-Bench, IFEval, and MMLU are English-centric. While multilingual variants exist (M-IFEval covers French instruction-following, and MMLU-ProX includes French translations), these are translated from English and may not capture French-specific linguistic nuances like politeness registers (tu vs. vous), which are critical in customer service tone evaluation.

The custom evaluation prompts would need to be written natively in French, not translated, because translated prompts tend to feel unnatural and may not trigger the same model behaviors. A professional customer complaint in French has different rhetorical conventions than an English one — French customers may express dissatisfaction more indirectly, and the expected tone of a professional response differs culturally. I would need a native French speaker, ideally with customer service experience, to write and validate both the prompts and the ground truth expectations.

The LLM-as-judge approach becomes particularly fragile in non-English contexts. Judge models like GPT-4o are primarily trained on English data and may have weaker calibration when evaluating French text quality. I would need to validate the judge by comparing its scores against French-speaking human evaluators on a calibration set, and I might need to adjust the judge prompt to explicitly define what "empathetic tone" means in French business communication rather than assuming English conventions transfer.

## Question 2: How do you respond to "Is this model AGI-level?"

This question needs careful reframing before it can be answered responsibly. "AGI-level" has no standard definition in the research community — it means different things depending on who you ask. Some define it as human-level performance across all cognitive tasks, others as the ability to learn new tasks without retraining, and some use it loosely to mean "very capable." The first step is to ask the client what outcome they are actually trying to assess: do they want to know if the model can handle all possible customer queries without human backup? Do they want to know if it can improve itself over time? Or are they asking whether it is "smart enough" for their use case?

For the chatbot evaluation specifically, I would redirect the conversation toward measurable capabilities. Rather than asking "is this AGI-level?", we would ask: "Can this model handle 95% of customer queries without escalation?" "Does it maintain accuracy across 20+ conversation turns?" "Can it correctly apply novel policy changes after they are added to the system prompt?" These are testable questions that provide actionable information.

If the client insists on the AGI framing, I would explain that no current model meets any serious definition of AGI, and that the term itself is more useful for marketing and research roadmaps than for procurement decisions. I would include strong caveats: the model is a tool optimized for next-token prediction, it does not "understand" customer needs in the way a human agent does, and its performance is bounded by its training data and prompt engineering. Any claims about AGI-level performance would be misleading to stakeholders and could create unrealistic expectations about the chatbot's autonomy and reliability.

## Question 3: What could you not evaluate without a human?

The one thing I could not evaluate without a human is whether the chatbot's response actually makes the customer feel heard and willing to continue doing business with the retailer. This is fundamentally different from whether the response is empathetic "on paper." An LLM judge can check whether the response includes empathy markers ("I understand your frustration"), follows policy, and provides next steps. But it cannot assess the felt experience of reading that response as a frustrated customer in a specific emotional state.

There is a gap between surface-level empathy signals and genuine emotional impact. A response might hit every checkbox — acknowledges the problem, apologizes, offers a solution — but still feel hollow or formulaic in a way that an LLM judge cannot detect because the judge does not experience emotions. A human evaluator who reads the response while roleplaying the customer's emotional state can catch these subtle failures: the response that is technically correct but feels cold, the apology that sounds automated, the transition from empathy to policy that feels too abrupt.

To incorporate human evaluation in practice, I would run a panel of 3–5 evaluators on a sample of 20–30 responses, asking them to rate both "did the response address my issue?" (functional quality) and "would I feel satisfied after reading this?" (emotional quality). The delta between these two scores reveals exactly where LLM-as-judge evaluation falls short. I would use this human data to calibrate and validate the LLM judge, not replace it — the goal is to build confidence that the automated evaluation correlates with real customer satisfaction, then use the LLM judge at scale with periodic human spot-checks.
