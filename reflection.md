# Question 1: What would change in your evaluation design if your client's data was in French?

If the retailer's customer data were in French, several parts of the evaluation would need rethinking, not just translating. First, the benchmark audit itself changes: MultiWoz, ABCD, and EchoMind are all English-language datasets, so their already-weak fit for my scenario would get even weaker — I'd need to look for French-language dialogue or customer-service datasets specifically, which are considerably rarer, meaning the "reject everything, build custom" conclusion from my audit would become even more clearly the right call.

Second, my custom evaluation prompts and ground-truth policy text would need to be written natively in French rather than translated from English, because translated prompts often carry over English sentence structure and idioms that a native French speaker would never actually use — this would make the test set less realistic and could cause the model being tested to perform artificially well or badly compared to real French customer interactions.

Third, and most importantly for my Step 4 judge design: tone and empathy norms are culturally specific. What counts as "appropriately empathetic but not over-apologetic" in French customer service conventions may differ meaningfully from English/American norms (formality register, use of "vous" vs "tu", how directness is perceived). I'd want either a native French speaker to help calibrate the judge's tone criteria, or to explicitly instruct the judge to evaluate against French customer-service norms rather than assuming Anglo-American defaults — otherwise I'd risk baking my own cultural assumptions into what looks like an objective rubric.

# Question 2: Your client asks "is this model AGI-level?" — how do you respond?

I'd start by clarifying that "AGI-level" isn't something my evaluation — or any evaluation I know of — is designed to answer, and that the term itself doesn't have a single agreed-upon technical definition even within the AI research community. Some people mean "matches human performance across virtually all cognitive tasks," others mean something narrower like "can learn new tasks without task-specific retraining." Without agreeing on what we're actually asking, any yes/no answer would be more misleading than helpful.

What I can honestly answer is much narrower and more useful to the client in practice: how well does this specific model perform on this specific task (complaint handling, policy accuracy) under these specific conditions. My evaluation showed the judge could reliably distinguish a good complaint response from a bad one, and that a well-constructed response scored highly — but that tells us nothing about the model's general reasoning, its performance on tasks outside retail customer service, or its behavior in genuinely novel situations it wasn't tested on.

I'd include a clear caveat that passing a narrow evaluation says nothing about broader capability, and that "AGI-level" claims require evidence across an enormous range of tasks, robustness checks, and long-horizon reasoning tests that are well outside the scope of what a 5-prompt custom evaluation (or honestly, what most current benchmarks) can establish. The honest answer is: "this model performs well at this task under these conditions — that's a much smaller and more defensible claim than AGI."

# Question 3: What is the one thing you could not evaluate without a human, and why?

The thing I'm least confident an LLM-as-judge (or a rule-based check) can fully replace is judging whether a response would actually land emotionally with a real, specific customer in the moment — not just whether it technically contains empathetic-sounding language, but whether it would genuinely make someone feel heard given their particular frustration, cultural background, and communication style. My own judge run illustrated this: it conflated "states the correct policy" with "no resolution," seemingly because the tone was cold — meaning it was picking up on something real (the response felt dismissive) but explaining it incorrectly (blaming the wrong criterion). A human reading the same response would likely have caught the actual nuance directly.

This matters because LLM judges evaluate text patterns associated with empathy (certain phrases, structure, acknowledgment language) rather than truly modeling how a specific human would feel receiving that message. Rule-based methods obviously can't touch this at all, since there's no keyword or structural pattern that reliably indicates "this actually sounds caring" versus "this uses the vocabulary of caring." An LLM-as-judge gets closer, but as my results showed, it can still misattribute why something feels wrong.

In practice, I'd incorporate human evaluation as a spot-check layer rather than the primary evaluation method: use the LLM judge for fast, cheap, at-scale screening (as I did here, at a fraction of a cent per case), but periodically sample real outputs for human review — especially for edge cases where the judge's score and reasoning don't seem to line up with each other, which is a signal (as we saw with the dismissive-response case) that something about the automated judgment might be off.
