SUBJECT: LLM Evaluation Results, Customer Service Chatbot

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

I built and ran an LLM-as-judge evaluation for complaint handling (Prompt #3 in `evaluation_design.md`), the single failure mode most tied to brand reputation and resolution rate: does the chatbot acknowledge an angry customer's specific issue, offer a concrete next step, and keep a calm tone. This memo covers that one scenario in depth; the other 4 prompts designed in `evaluation_design.md` (return eligibility, order status honesty, product knowledge limits, policy bypass resistance) are designed but not yet implemented and run.

METHODOLOGY

Rather than relying on general-purpose benchmarks such as MMLU, which test academic knowledge and do not reflect customer service quality, I built a structured LLM-as-judge prompt (task description, 3 explicit criteria, reasoning steps, JSON output) and used it to score chatbot responses to angry complaints, with gpt-4o-mini as both the response generator and the judge, at temperature 0.

The test set had 5 cases: 2 responses generated live by the model in response to real complaint prompts, and 3 fixed responses written with a known, controlled quality (good, bad, borderline), used to check the judge's own reliability before trusting its scores on live model output.

RESULTS

The calibration check (a score within 1 point of the expected value counts as a pass) matched exactly on all 3 controlled cases: the known-good response scored 5/5, the known-bad response scored 1/5, and the known-borderline response scored 3/5. The 2 live-generated responses scored 5/5 and 4/5; the 4/5 case failed only the tone criterion despite correctly acknowledging the issue and offering a resolution, a useful edge case to keep for future calibration work. Average score across all 5 cases was 3.6/5. Full scores, reasoning, and timing/token data are in `evaluation_results.json`, generated by `llm_judge_evaluation.ipynb`.

CAVEATS & LIMITATIONS

This evaluation covers one scenario (complaint tone) with 5 hand-written test cases and a single run per case, so it says nothing yet about the other 4 designed prompts, and cannot be treated as statistically representative of full production traffic. LLM judge scores carry inherent variance between runs, even at temperature 0. The judge itself is an LLM and shares many of the same blind spots as the model it is grading, most notably a preference for longer, more formally worded responses (see the bias analysis in `evaluation_design.md`), so a low judge score does not always mean a genuinely bad response, and a high one does not guarantee a real customer would feel well served. I also have no visibility into contamination, I do not know if this exact wording overlaps with data the model was trained on. These results should be treated as a first-pass signal to guide further testing, not a final quality certification.

RECOMMENDATION

Under these conditions, for complaint-style responses specifically, gpt-4o-mini's replies were judged strong on acknowledgment and resolution, with one live case flagged only for tone. Before a production rollout, I would recommend implementing and running the other 4 designed prompts (return eligibility, order status honesty, product knowledge limits, policy bypass resistance), each with its own judge or rule-based check, since none of those have been tested yet. Confidence in this recommendation is low to moderate, given the narrow, single-scenario scope of what has actually been run so far.

ADDITIONAL METRICS

The 5 judge calls took under 9 seconds total and cost well under a tenth of a cent using gpt-4o-mini; this covers the judge only, the 2 live response-generation calls were not separately timed or costed in this run. Full per-case timing, token, and cost data for the judge calls is in `evaluation_results.json`. At this rate, cost is not a barrier to running the remaining 4 prompt types, or a much larger test set, before deployment.
